This paper introduces a methodology for the detection of dislocation defects in silicon carbide (SiC) substrates
through the application of photoluminescence (PL) imaging technology in conjunction with the you only look
once (YOLO) object detection algorithm. Dislocation defects in 4H-SiC substrates have a considerable influence
on the device performance, thereby necessitating accurate and rapid detection methods. However, the identi-
fication of these defects in PL images is often complicated by their low contrast and indistinct boundaries. In this
study, image enhancement methods were applied to improve the quality of PL images, while the YOLO11-OBB
(Oriented Bounding Box) model was used to effectively detect and localize basal plane dislocations (BPDs) in 4H-
SiC substrates. The experimental results demonstrated notable improvements in defect detection and localiza-
tion. Additionally, the use of the class-balanced multi-sampling (CBMS) technique enhances the distribution of
classes within the dataset, thereby increasing the model’s ability to identify minority defects such as BPDs. This
study  offers  an  innovative  strategy  for  the  efficient  nondestructive  detection  of  dislocation  defects  in  4H-SiC
substrates  and  highlights  the  advantages  of  combining  deep  learning  techniques  with  image  enhancement
methods.

1. Introduction

Silicon  carbide  (SiC)  material  has  excellent  performance,  in  high-
power electronic devices, radio frequency uses, and high-temperature
Settings  are  widely  used  in  these  aspects.  In  the  field  of  power  elec-
tronics and the emerging energy industry, the demand for SiC substrates
has increased greatly, but dislocation defects exist in the SiC substrate,
that  is,  the  base  surface  dislocation.  These  defects  have  a  negative
impact on the function of the device and may even cause the breakdown
of the device [1,2]. Therefore, it is critical to detect and characterize
these defects in a timely and accurate manner during the production of
SiC substrates.

Traditional  dislocation  detection  techniques,  such  as  transmission
electron microscopy (TEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and other similar
methods [3–5], are typically destructive and are not suitable for in-line
detection in manufacturing processes. To address this issue, this study
primarily  concentrates  on  non-invasive  testing  techniques,  with  a
particular  emphasis  on  photoluminescence  (PL)  technology,  which
stands  out  because  of  its  high  sensitivity,  nondestructive  nature,  and

superior spatial resolution [6,7]. The PL method involves exciting the
4H-SiC substrate to generate spectral signals, which results in variations
in the brightness or tone of the PL images, indicative of dislocation de-
fects.  By  observing  these  variations,  much  key  data  can  be  obtained,
which helps detect defects in 4H-SiC materials [8]. Nonetheless, the high
doping density of the 4H-SiC substrate often presents challenges in PL
imaging, such as the low contrast of defects and indistinct boundaries
[9].  These  issues  complicate  the  accurate  localization  and  character-
ization of defects. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop novel
techniques  that  can  effectively  detect  defects  while  preserving  the
integrity of the images produced.

In  recent  years,  significant  advancements  have  been  achieved  in
deep learning. Specifically, in the realm of object detection technology,
these advancements have led to the development of novel approaches
for  automatic defect  detection.  The  you only  look once  (YOLO) algo-
rithm,  known  for  its  robust  real-time  performance  and  precise  locali-
zation  capabilities,  has  gained  widespread  application  in  object
detection. This effectively facilitates the target localization and classi-
fication. Compared to conventional object detection techniques, YOLO

Although the YOLO model has been widely used in the detection of
metal surface defects [14–16], its effectiveness in analyzing PL images of
4H-SiC  has  yet  to  be  established,  which  presents  a  distinct  challenge
compared to metal surfaces. The 4H-SiC substrates face specific obsta-
cles  in  PL  imaging,  such  as  low  image  contrast  due  to  high  doping
concentrations [9]. Furthermore, the spectral variations associated with
dislocation defects are often subtle, compounded by background noise
and irregular brightness fluctuations, complicating the detection process
and potentially undermining the model’s reliability [17–19]. Therefore,
applying  YOLO  to  4H-SiC  PL  images  requires  addressing  these  chal-
lenges and customizing the model accordingly.

To overcome these limitations, this study presents a novel approach
for  identifying  dislocation  defects  in  4H-SiC  substrates.  The  proposed
method leverages PL imaging technology in conjunction with the YOLO
target-detection algorithm. By employing advanced image enhancement
techniques, this method enhances the quality of the PL images and in-
tegrates the YOLO11 target detection algorithm, which can accurately
identify and locate basal plane dislocations (BPDs) within the 4H-SiC
substrate.  Notably,  the  YOLO11  model  incorporates  a  directional
bounding  box  feature,  allowing  it  to  effectively  manage  defects  char-
acterized  by  rotation  and  irregular  shapes,  thereby  significantly
enhancing  detection  accuracy.  In  addition,  this  study  uses  data  pre-
processing  techniques,  namely  class-balanced  multi-sampling  (CBMS)
technology,  to  alleviate  class  imbalance  issues  in  the  dataset  before
performing object detection, with the aim of improving the stability and
overall performance of the detection model.

The research methodology described in this paper proceeds as fol-
lows. Initially, PL images of a 4H-SiC substrate were captured using a
self-designed and built PL Imager System. Subsequently, the image was
preprocessed using a series of image enhancement techniques, including
Gaussian speckle homogenization, brightness and contrast adjustment,
and denoising. The purpose of these preprocessing steps is to improve
the visibility of defects and detection accuracy. Ultimately, the YOLO11-
OBB model was employed in conjunction with CBMS for target detec-
tion, enabling the precise identification of dislocation defects and their
respective locations. This approach demonstrates the potential of inte-
grating image enhancement methods with deep learning-based object
detection techniques to effectively identify dislocation defects in 4H-SiC
substrates, thereby validating the efficacy of the proposed method.

2.1.2. PL image acquisition

The setup of the imaging system is illustrated in Fig. 1. The specimen
was  positioned  on  a  motorized  X-Y-Z  displacement  stage  with  a  high
precision (positioning accuracy ≤ 2 μm). Imaging was performed using a
stepwise fixed-point photography approach with each step involving a
displacement  of  50 μm in  a  single  direction.  The  imaging  system  was
positioned  perpendicular  to  the  displacement  stage  and  a  high-
sensitivity industrial camera was used for image acquisition. The cam-
era  featured  a  resolution  of  4096 × 3000 pixels  and  a  pixel  size  of
3.45 μm × 3.45 μm, facilitating the effective capture of intricate surface
details  of  the  sample.  Moreover,  the  camera  exhibited  sensitivity  to
infrared light with wavelengths of ≥ 700 nm, rendering it suitable for
the infrared imaging requirements of the experiment.

The  camera  utilizes  dual  telecentric  lenses  and  a  long-wavelength
pass  filter  (≥ 700 nm)  to  exclusively  permit  the  transmission  of
infrared light. The optical setup incorporates LBTEK optical elements. A
light  shield  encloses  the  imaging  optical  pathway  to  reduce  external
light  disturbances  and  enable  precise  imaging.  A  20X  magnification
infrared objective lens was affixed to the system’s base to improve the
image resolution. Imaging was performed at ambient temperature with a
1-second exposure duration to capture high-fidelity image data from a
specified area (2.048 × 1.5 mm2).

The experimental computing platform utilized a workstation equip-
ped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, which offered ample computational
capacity for analyzing extensive PL image datasets. Training was per-
formed using the PyTorch deep-learning framework on Windows OS.

2.2.3.

Image quality metrics (IQM) program

To  ensure  precise  evaluation  results by  focusing on key  areas and
minimizing  background  noise  interference,  all  IQMs  were  computed
using image data within the designated IQM calculation region. Given
the distinct manifestations of threading dislocations (TDs) and BPDs in
PL images (TDs as dark spots and BPDs as dark line structures) evalua-
tion algorithms have been tailored for dark spot and dark line images to
account for these specific defect characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 2.
The specific workflow is described below.

Both programs adopted a local regional evaluation strategy. The IQM
program  for  dark  spots  first  identifies  a  single  defect  (Fig.  3(a))  and

marking the locations of the dark spot with red dots (Fig. 3(b)), then
extracts its defect contour from the enhanced PL image, as indicated by
the green box in Fig. 3(c). Next, based on the center of the extracted
defect  contour,  a  50 × 50 pixel  square  IQM  calculation  region  was
expanded, as shown by the red box in Fig. 3(c). Subsequently, the same
contour and IQM calculation regions are marked at the same location in
the  original  image  Fig.  3(d).  Finally,  the  calculation  of  each  image
quality  evaluation  metric  is  completed  within  the  IQM  calculation
region.

The IQM  program  for  dark lines  first selects  a  50 × 200 pixel  IQM
calculation region, as shown by the blue box in Fig. 4(b). The dark line is
then  identified  within  the  IQM  calculation  region  (the  yellow  line  in
Fig. 4(c)). Next, it displays and confirms the selected calculation region
and dark line in the enhanced PL image (the red box and yellow line in
Fig. 4(d)). Finally, the same dark line and IQM calculation region were
marked on the original PL image (red box and green lines in Fig. 4(e)),
and the image quality metrics for each image are calculated within the
calculation region. To guarantee the representativeness and statistical
reliability of the evaluation outcomes, 100 samples were gathered for
each defect type. A thorough and precise assessment of image quality
can  be  achieved  by  analyzing  the  variations  in  the  metrics  of  defects
located in the same area.

3. Results and discussion

3.1. Comparison and analysis of image enhancement effects

3.1.1. Dislocation defect resolution and localization in PL images

Previous  research  has  demonstrated  that  dislocation  defects  in  PL
images  appear as  circular dark spots known  as TDs and  dark lines of
varying  lengths  referred  to  as  BPDs  [25].  Owing  to  the  high  doping
concentration of the substrate, enhancing the PL images is often neces-
sary for clear visualization of dislocation defects. As shown in Fig. 6, the
enhanced  images  significantly  improve  the  recognition  of  dislocation
defects. The original PL images (Fig. 6(a)) show an uneven light distri-
bution due to Gaussian spots, which obscure the defect regions. After the
image enhancement, the Gaussian spots were homogenized, resulting in

a uniform light distribution and more prominent dislocation defect re-
gions (Fig. 6(b)). Further denoising significantly reduces the noise and
enhances the prominence of the dislocation regions (Fig. 6(c)). Finally,
the  etching  image  (Fig.  6(d))  was  used  for  further  classification  and
localization of the dislocation defects in the PL images.

Through  defect  classification  and  localization  analysis  of  the  PL
image,  it  was  found  that  the  PL  image  containing  BPD  (purple  box)
shows dark lines (yellow dashed box) similar in shape to BPD; however,
no corresponding BPD etch pits can be found in the etched image. Ac-
cording to previous research, dislocation defects are considered to be
growth defects [41], and their depth in the wafer can influence the re-
sults of both PL and etch detection. It is speculated that the mismatch
between the PL image and the etch results may be due to differences in
the etch depth and PL penetration depth. To verify this hypothesis, the
wafer  thickness  was  measured  before  and  after  etching.  The  results
showed that under the current etching parameters, the etch depth was

3.2. Comparison and analysis of object detection methods

3.2.1. Comparison of the impact of bounding boxes on performance

The  original  version  of  YOLO11  uses  axis-aligned  bounding  boxes
(AABB).  In  this  case,  the  bounding  box  surrounding  the  target  was
aligned with the coordinate axis. However, this axis-aligned bounding
box is unsuitable for rotating or irregular objects. As shown in Fig. 10(a),
when the target is tilted at a large angle, the detection region contains
excessive  background,  making  target  recognition  and  classification
difficult and reducing target recognition accuracy. In contrast, the ori-
ented bounding box (OBB), which is more sensitive to direction, is more
effective when dealing with rotating objects or those that are not aligned
with  the  axis,  and  can  provide  more  accurate  target  positioning  and
detection results [42]. In this work, the PL signal of BPD is linear, and
the image contains objects with varying rotation angles. Therefore, we
replaced the AABB in YOLO11 with OBB, as shown in Fig. 10(b). This
allows BPD to be more accurately outlined while reducing background
in  the  detection  area  and  lowering  the  probability  of  bounding  box
overlap.

To  evaluate  the  performance  differences  between  YOLO11  and
YOLO11-OBB in object-detection tasks, a comparative experiment was
conducted, and the results are presented in Fig. 11.

From  Fig.  11,  it  can  be  observed  that  YOLO11  and  YOLO11-OBB
exhibit different performance. In terms of the trends in precision and
recall, the YOLO11-OBB model (blue curve) showed a similar trend to
YOLO11 (red curve) during most of the training process, but its precision
gradually increased after approximately 50 epochs. However, it is still
not possible to directly determine the impact of OBB on performance
from these charts, as these individual metrics alone do not reflect the
overall effect. Therefore, the precision–recall (PR) curve can serve as an
important basis for further evaluating the strengths and weaknesses of
the two models, as shown in Fig. 12 below.

From the PR curve (Fig. 12), it can be seen that BPDs maintain higher
precision at higher recall rates, indicating that OBB plays a crucial role
in  detecting  elongated  dark-line  defects  (BPDs).  This  is  because  elon-
gated dark line defects require more precise bounding box localization,
and OBB provides higher localization accuracy, thereby improving the
detection precision. The red curve represents the detection results for
circular dark spot defects (TDs). The localization requirements for these
defects are relatively low and they are less sensitive to the rotation angle
of the bounding box. Consequently, the advantages of using OBB are not
as pronounced, leading to lower precision at higher recall rates. Because
the TDs themselves do not require precise bounding boxes, the OBB does
not provide significant performance improvement.

3.2.3. Data optimization method - class balanced multi-sampling (CBMS)
Dislocation  defects  in  wafers  are  unevenly  distributed,  and  the
number of different types of dislocations varies greatly. According to our
self-built dataset, the number of TDs in the image is much higher than
that of BPDs. If no processing was done, the YOLO model might focus too
much on the frequently occurring TDs during training and ignore the
BPDs, thereby affecting the detection accuracy of BPDs. To address this

issue, we preprocess the data using the CBMS method before performing
object detection. This method adjusts the sampling probabilities of each
category to make the training samples more balanced, avoiding model
learning  imbalances  caused  by  an  excess  or  deficiency  of  samples  in
certain  categories  [46,47].  In  this  work,  we  employ  random  over-
sampling  of  BPD  with  1000  images  to  increase  the  frequency  of  this
category.

Fig.  17 shows  the  performance  improvement  of  the  YOLOv8-OBB
and YOLO11-OBB models after the introduction of the CBMS strategy,
comparing the training curves of the different models.

Based on the results shown in Fig. 17, it is evident that the intro-
duction of the CBMS strategy significantly improved the performance of
the  YOLOv8-OBB  and  YOLO11-OBB  models  across  all  metrics.  In  the
precision,  recall,  mAP50,  and  mAP50-90  curves,  models  using  CBMS
(blue  and  purple  lines)  demonstrated  more  stable  and  higher  perfor-
mance than those without CBMS (red and green lines).

In  the  early  stages  of  training,  the  YOLOv8-OBB  (CBMS)  and
YOLO11-OBB (CBMS) models showed rapid and consistent increases in
precision  and  recall.  Their  curves  demonstrate  elevated  values  and  a
more gradual increase, suggesting that the models are more proficient in
learning  from  and  managing  data  throughout  the  training  process.
Conversely, the YOLOv8-OBB and YOLO11-OBB models lacking CBMS
showed more significant variations in recall and mAP metrics, accom-
panied  by  a  slower  rate  of  convergence.  Moreover,  YOLO11-OBB
(CBMS)  has  always  been  better  than  YOLOv8-OBB  (CBMS)  for  every
indicator, indicating that the YOLO11 architecture receives more ben-
efits from the CBMS strategy.

Based  on  the  impact  of  algorithmic  structure  on  performance  dif-
ferences in previous models, the results of this experiment indicate that
data preprocessing prior to object detection plays a more prominent role
in improving model performance than optimizing algorithm structure.
This  can  be  explained  from  several  perspectives:  first,  deep  learning
models are highly dependent on data distribution. Neural networks are
essentially  “statistical  learners”  that  learn  patterns  and  distributions
from training data. If the data itself has an imbalanced class distribution,
the model may overfit the frequently occurring classes while neglecting
rare classes, leading to performance bias. Second, object detection relies
more heavily on the ratio of positive to negative samples than classifi-
cation. Object detection not only classifies objects but also locates their
bounding boxes. Each training sample is typically divided into “positive
samples”  and  “a  large  number  of  negative  samples”  (background).  If
positive  samples  for  certain  categories  are  extremely  scarce,  the  re-
gressor will not learn sufficient bounding box regression signals, and the
classifier will struggle to capture category features (low recall, high false
negatives)  [46–48].  CBMS  ensures  that  each  category  has  a  more
balanced probability of appearing during training, enabling the model to
learn the discriminative features of each category more fairly, improve
recall for low-frequency categories, and reduce training bias and over-
fitting  for  common  categories.  Among  these,  improving  recall  is

4. Conclusions

This  study introduces a  novel approach for identifying  dislocation
defects  in  4H-SiC  substrates  by  employing  PL  imaging  techniques
alongside  the  YOLO  object  detection  algorithm.  Due  to  the  fact  that
